# Priority Question Answer

Question:
Which states are next after Texas, and do we hold licensed rights to publish their operator and production data?

Answer:
This is not locked, and it isn't ours to answer. Both halves are Ryan's decision — and on the data-rights half, only Ryan and the data science team can answer it. Ryan's decision is final.

On the sequence: no state order has been decided. Any ordering appearing in our working documents is illustrative — used to show the architecture can take another state without a rebuild — not a proposal about which state comes next or when. That is a commercial and product decision.

On licensed rights: we have no visibility into how each dataset was sourced or what terms attach to it. The distinction that matters is between data published by state regulators as public record, and data obtained from a vendor under licence — where redistribution can be restricted even when the underlying facts are public. Only the data science team knows which of our datasets came by which route, and only Ryan can decide what we publish on that basis.

What we can offer as input — clearly marked as current understanding, not established fact:

What we're reasonably confident of: Texas county appraisal districts appraise each owner's producing mineral interest under Property Tax Code §23.175. This is a statutory mechanism, and the strongest evidence for it is simply that we already hold the resulting data.

What is only our current reading, and should not be treated as settled: in looking at New Mexico, Oklahoma, North Dakota, Colorado and Wyoming, we did not find an equivalent statewide owner-level roll produced the way Texas produces one — the tax and valuation mechanisms appear to differ (gross production tax in place of property tax; valuation at operator or leasehold level; unit-level assessment).

That finding has real limits, and they should be stated plainly:

It says we did not find an equivalent statewide appraisal roll. It does not establish that owner-level data is unobtainable in those states.
Owner interests may well be assembleable by other routes — county deed and courthouse records, division orders, lease and assignment filings, probate records, state land office records, or commercial data vendors. We have not investigated those routes.
Someone with real depth in the oil and gas industry may simply know where this data lives and how to put it together. That kind of practitioner knowledge is not something desk research surfaces, and it would override our reading. This is exactly the sort of question worth putting to the data science team and to anyone with hands-on industry experience before it is treated as a constraint.
The reassuring part: the architecture does not depend on the answer. Graceful degradation means a state's pages exist with whatever data that state has — an owner section appears where owner data exists and is simply absent where it doesn't. So if owner data turns out to be obtainable in another state, it slots in as a data load with no restructuring. If it doesn't, nothing breaks. We are not betting the structure on our current understanding being correct, which is the right posture given how provisional it is.

One suggestion for whoever owns it: when a state is chosen, recording one line per state — what data we can get, its source, whether public record or licensed, and if licensed whether redistribution is permitted — would make the publication decision documented and straightforward. Happy to compile that table with the data science team's input.
