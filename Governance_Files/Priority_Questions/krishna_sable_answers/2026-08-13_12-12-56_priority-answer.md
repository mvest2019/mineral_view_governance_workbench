# Priority Question Answer

Question:
Does publicly ranking or comparing operator performance need legal review before it stays public?

Answer:
I can't clear this one — I'm not able to give a legal opinion, and it would be wrong to imply otherwise. What I can do is set out the actual exposure so a review, if commissioned, is short and well-aimed.

What the feature set is: three public pages — compare operators, compare operator performance, and operator presentations. Six months of analytics show each drawing roughly 7–10 users a month.

What lowers the risk:

The underlying data is public regulatory record — Railroad Commission production filings, permits, well records. We are computing on published facts, not private information.
Factual, sourced comparison of public data is ordinary practice in this market and in most industries.
What raises it:

Comparative rankings name identifiable companies, and companies are more litigious about reputation than individuals are.
Presentation matters more than data. A neutral metric ("barrels produced, 2025, per RRC filings") reads very differently from an implied judgment ("worst performing operator").
Stale or opaque numbers are the real hazard. If an operator disputes a figure and we can't show the source, the as-of date, and the method, the position is weak regardless of the data being public.
Practical mitigations, worth doing whether or not a review happens:

Label every metric with its source and as-of date.
Publish the methodology — how each figure is computed.
Keep framing factual and neutral; present data, not verdicts.
Provide a correction route so a disputed figure has somewhere to go other than a lawyer.
My honest recommendation: if these pages stay public and comparative, a short legal review is cheap insurance and would let us keep them with confidence rather than caution.

One thing that may make it simpler: whether these pages stay public at all is still an open architecture decision — keep public, remove, or move into the portal. At 7–10 users a month, the public versions are carrying very little traffic. Moving them inside the portal would serve the users who value them, simplify the public architecture, and reduce this exposure at the same time. That's Ryan's call on product grounds, but the three considerations happen to point the same way.
